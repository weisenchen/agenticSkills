## 1. Openings: what to do, what not to do

### 1.1 ⛔ Don't: open with a joke

> `[00:04:39.530]` "I don't recommend it, and the reason"
> `[00:04:44.300]` "is that, in the beginning of a talk,"
> `[00:04:46.820]` "people are still putting their laptops away."
> `[00:04:49.420]` "They're becoming adjusted to your speaking parameters,"
> `[00:04:52.250]` "to your vocal parameters, and they're not ready for a joke."
> `[00:05:00.090]` "They usually fall flat."

**How to fix it**: no gag in the first 30 seconds that needs an audience already on board; keep
the joke for §2.3 (you may tell one at the end).
**Note**: this is a **live-lecture** rule. A finished video has no moment where the audience is
putting its laptops away — see the **manual mapping** in §8.1 for the video-side criterion.

### 1.2 ✅ Do: open with an **empowerment promise**

> `[00:05:02.420]` "What you want to do instead is start with empowerment promise."
> `[00:05:09.560]` "You want to tell people what they're"
> `[00:05:11.060]` "going to know at the end of the hour"
> `[00:05:13.660]` "that they didn't know at the beginning of the hour."
> `[00:05:17.808]` "It's the reason for being here."

**Template (his own demonstration)**: `[00:05:24.230]` "At the end of this 60 minutes, you will know things"

**Executable**: the first sentence or first paragraph answers "what will they know at the end
that they don't know now". **His promise also carries a probability statement and a non-linear
delivery criterion**:

> `[00:02:32.810]` "that some one of those examples, some heuristic, some technique,"
> `[00:02:37.640]` "maybe only one, will make--"
> `[00:02:40.040]` "will be the one that gets you the job."

→ you do not have to promise "all of it will be useful". Saying out loud "one of these will be
the one that gets you the job" is more credible, not less.

### 1.3 ✅ Fine: open with a **cross-domain analogy** (his own opening move)

> `[00:00:19.220]` "PATRICK WINSTON: The Uniform Code of Military Justice"
> `[00:00:30.620]` "There ought to be a similar protection for students"

**Action**: land the abstract concept in an everyday or foreign-domain picture first.
**Analogy first, thesis second.**

### 1.4 ✅ In-person only: set a **rule of engagement** up front and enforce it

> `[00:03:14.300]` "to have a rule of engagement, and that"
> `[00:03:31.800]` "and the answer is, we humans only"
> `[00:03:34.830]` "have one language processor."
> `[00:03:48.030]` "And worse yet, you distract all of the people around you."

**Why**: humans have **one language processor** — occupy it with a web page and the offender
drops out, and so does everyone sitting around them; and if someone in the room has a laptop
open, the speaker performs worse as well (`[00:03:52.570]` "And worse yet, if I see a open laptop somewhere back" → `[00:04:08.520]` "that they came to have.").
★ This mechanism is **the substrate of the whole lecture**: §3.5 crime ⑤ (too many words on a
slide) is the same mechanism seen from another side.

### 1.5 Opening checklist (follow it)

| # | Action | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Don't open with a joke | `[00:04:39.530]` |
| 2 | First sentence = empowerment promise: what they will know at the end that they don't know now | `[00:05:02.420]` |
| 3 | The promise may carry the non-linear criterion "one of these will make a difference" | `[00:02:32.810]` |
| 4 | You may open with one cross-domain analogy | `[00:00:19.220]` |
| 5 | In person only: set the rules (laptops closed, phones away) and say why | `[00:03:34.830]` |

## 2. Closings: what to do, what not to do

### 2.1 ⛔ Don't: make conclusions / "thank you for listening" / a long collaborator list / a how-to-ask-questions page your final screen

**The correct label for the final screen is `contributions` (what you have done)**:

> `[00:55:38.340]` "If you say these are my conclusions,"
> `[00:55:44.910]` "What they care about is what you have done."
> `[00:55:47.557]` "And that's why your final slide should"

**The four rejected final screens, one by one**:

| Wrong final screen | His words | How to fix it |
|---|---|---|
| A long list of collaborators | `[00:54:03.810]` "at the end of a talk, it's a kind of let down"; `[00:54:16.970]` "Not on the last slide, on the first slide." | Put collaborators on the **first** slide |
| A blank "thank you for listening" slide | `[00:54:31.060]` "This is the worst possible way to end a talk."; `[00:54:40.660]` "It squanders real estate." | Replace it with contributions |
| A how-to-ask-questions slide | `[00:54:56.780]` "I often see it."; `[00:55:01.300]` "Also, it wastes opportunity." | Delete it; say it out loud |
| conclusions | `[00:55:41.040]` "these are perfectly legitimate conclusions" / `[00:55:42.960]` "that nobody cares about." | Relabel it contributions |

**His own contributions slide**: `[00:56:13.020]` "Yes, here are the things that I typically demonstrate." + `[00:56:23.146]` "And the final element there is this is what we get out of it,"; and
`[00:56:21.160]` "And I wait for people to read it." (give them time to read it).

### 2.2 ⛔ Don't: end your last sentence on "thank you"

> `[00:57:55.650]` "It's a weak move."
> `[00:58:08.460]` "it suggests that everybody has stayed"
> `[00:58:20.430]` "So once wild applause has started,"
> `[00:58:25.380]` "But the last thing you do should not be saying thank you."

He anticipates and rejects the "but everybody says it" objection: `[00:58:33.570]` "Well, what everybody does is not necessarily the right thing."

### 2.3 ✅ Do: a joke **is** allowed at the end (the exact opposite of the opening)

> `[00:56:59.840]` "It's OK."
> `[00:57:02.232]` "By the time you're done, people have adjusted themselves"
> `[00:57:05.728]` "They're ready for a joke."

**Mechanism**: the same reason as §1.1, seen from the other side — by now the audience has
adapted to your vocal parameters.

### 2.4 ✅ Do: give an explicit **end signal** (not a courtesy line)

> `[01:01:47.280]` "Those are conventions that tell you that the event is over."

Three forms he accepts:

1. **benediction**: `[00:59:18.190]` "God bless you, and God bless America." (`[00:59:23.241]` "PATRICK WINSTON: So that's a classic benediction ending.").
2. **salute the audience**: `[01:02:00.180]` "is that you can salute the audience." + `[01:02:07.320]` "And by that, I mean, you can say something".
3. **conventional action**: e.g. at a concert you know it is time to clap when the conductor
   shakes hands with the concert master (`[01:01:42.230]` "but rather when the conductor goes over and shakes hands"); he even freeze-frames a politician
   who `[01:00:15.130]` "he seems to be almost pressing his lips together, forcing" and `[01:00:20.315]` "Then there's another place where he does a little salute.".

### 2.5 Closing checklist (follow it)

| # | Action | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The final screen says **contributions** only — not conclusions / thank-you / how-to-ask / a collaborator list | `[00:55:47.557]` |
| 2 | Collaborator credits go on the **first** screen | `[00:54:16.970]` |
| 3 | **Do not end on "thank you"** (once applause starts you may mouth it) | `[00:57:55.650]` |
| 4 | A joke **is** allowed at the end (it is not at the start) | `[00:56:59.840]` |
| 5 | End with an "event is over" signal: benediction / salute the audience / conventional action | `[01:01:47.280]` |

## 3. Board / props / slides

### 3.1 The general principle: pick the tool by **purpose**, not "slides are bad"

> `[00:13:41.230]` "I believe that this is the right tool for speaking"
> `[00:13:52.330]` "The slides are good when your purpose is exposing,"

| Purpose | Tool | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| **informing** (teaching, explaining, lecturing) | **board / write it out stroke by stroke** | `[00:13:56.290]` "but this is what I use when I'm informing, teaching, lecturing," |
| **exposing** (showing off an idea, job talk, conference report) | slides are fine | `[00:13:52.330]` "The slides are good when your purpose is exposing," |

### 3.2 Why the board is good (he names each virtue)

| Virtue | His words | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **graphic quality** | `[00:14:04.480]` "you have a graphic quality." | draw while you talk, don't just show the finished diagram |
| **speed property** — writing speed ≈ absorption speed | `[00:14:30.310]` "The speed with which you write on the blackboard"; `[00:14:39.040]` "nobody can go that fast." | match delivery rate to absorption rate (**this is the only rate benchmark he ever gives**) |
| **target** — something to point at | `[00:14:47.260]` "is that it can be a target."; `[00:14:59.200]` "find themselves suddenly aware of their hands."; `[00:15:59.210]` "that it has to do with whether you're concealing a weapon." | don't put your hands in your pockets or behind your back (in some cultures that reads as offensive / like concealing a weapon); give your hands somewhere to be |

He even gives the extreme version of "pointing at the board": Seymour Papert `[00:16:29.880]` "And what I discovered is that Papert was constantly",
while `[00:16:35.360]` "and I noted that none of the stuff he was pointing to" — and yet `[00:16:43.190]` "Nevertheless, it was an effective technique.".

### 3.3 Props: the masters of the craft are playwrights

> `[00:16:56.850]` "The custodians of knowledge about props"
> `[00:30:23.250]` "It's amazing how props tend to be"

Hedda Gabler's stove (Ibsen): `[00:17:54.330]` "But the potbellied stove is always there," → `[00:18:13.890]` "and finally all consuming, and you just".
**Action**: let one object run through the whole piece, let tension grow along with it, and spend
it at the end. That is the prop he remembers for a lifetime (`[00:18:21.210]` "This memorable thing is what I remember about the play.").

### 3.4 ★ The mechanism: **empathetic mirroring** (his self-described lunatic-fringe view)

> `[00:22:42.870]` "It has to do with what I would call empathetic mirroring."
> `[00:23:05.020]` "all those little mirror neurons in your head, I believe,"
> `[00:23:28.190]` "You need to see it in the physical world."

**The most transferable clause in this section**: an audience does not remember "what you showed
them", it remembers "the action it performed along with you". → whenever people can **do it with
you**, don't just **show it to them**: prop > picture > slide.

### 3.5 ★ The **nine crimes** of slides (each: what's wrong + how to fix it)

| Crime | What's wrong (his words) | How to fix it |
|---|---|---|
| **① Too many slides, too many words per slide** | `[00:25:08.610]` "You have too many, and they have too many words."; `[00:25:19.800]` "My reply was, because it's always true." / `[00:25:22.770]` "There are always too many slides, always too many words." | Accept that this is the **general case**, not the exception — then cut slide by slide |
| **② Reading your slides** | `[00:25:48.510]` "one of the things you shouldn't do is read your transparencies."; `[00:25:51.342]` "People in your audience know how to read,"; `[00:25:53.050]` "and reading will just annoy them." | Don't read them; keep only a few words per slide and keep them readable (`[00:25:56.940]` "have only a few words on each transparency,") |
| **③ Speaker at one end, screen at the other** | `[00:26:16.560]` "So you get into this tennis match feeling"; `[00:26:24.600]` "You want the slides to be condiments to what" | Slides are **condiments**, the speaker is the main course: stand next to the screen, don't make the audience watch tennis |
| **④ Background junk / logos / title** | `[00:26:33.420]` "Step number one is to get rid of the background junk."; `[00:27:05.310]` "Well, we can get rid of the logos."; `[00:27:15.400]` "Get rid of the title." | Delete in four steps: background → words → logo → title (say the title out loud instead, `[00:27:21.250]` "I'm telling you the title.") |
| **⑤ Many words mean the audience reads and stops listening** (the hardest mechanism) | `[00:27:32.295]` "I mentioned it before-- we-- have only one language"; controlled-experiment result `[00:28:21.260]` "And the answer is, what they read on the slide."; subject's own words `[00:28:33.190]` "said, I wish you hadn't talked so much." | **Take the words you intend to say off the screen**; leave only anchors on it |
| **⑥ Font too small** (the root cause of "too many words") | `[00:28:51.780]` "of a crime Microsoft has committed" / `[00:28:55.410]` "by allowing you to use fonts that are too small." | Keep one sample slide to measure your **lower legibility bound**; the in-room answer was 40–50 pt — `[00:29:27.050]` "35 is beginning to get too small, not necessarily" |
| **⑦ Laser pointer** (loss of eye contact) | `[00:31:17.940]` "You can't use a laser pointer without turning your head"; `[00:31:25.050]` "you lose contact with the audience."; `[00:31:01.440]` "I have no eye contact, no engagement, nothing." — and a student watching a talk with him said `[00:31:08.300]` "and she said, you know what, we could all leave," `[00:31:11.790]` "and he wouldn't know." | **Don't use a laser pointer**: `[00:31:36.840]` "Put a little arrow on there and say," |
| **⑧ Too heavy overall** (no air) | `[00:31:52.480]` "ask me to review a presentation, I"; `[00:32:04.450]` "too heavy, too much text, not enough air, not" | Review method: **print it out and lay it on a table** to see whether there is air; on slides that carry text, **stop and give people time to read** (`[00:32:52.930]` "but when I come to those, I give the audience time to read them.") |
| **⑨ `hapax legomenon`** (a deliberately incomprehensible graphic) | `[00:33:28.770]` "What that means is, this is the kind of slide"; `[00:33:49.640]` "It's something you in the audience can't understand," | **Exactly one per presentation**, and say out loud that not-understanding is the point |

### 3.6 Tool checklist (follow it)

| # | Action | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ask the purpose first: informing → board; exposing → slides | `[00:13:41.230]` |
| 2 | If people can do it with you, don't just show it: prop > picture > slide | `[00:23:05.020]` |
| 3 | Slides are a condiment, not the main event; attention follows the speaker | `[00:26:24.600]` |
| 4 | Four-step delete on every slide: background → words → logo → title | `[00:26:33.420]`–`[00:27:15.400]` |
| 5 | Font floor ≈ 40–50 pt (35 is already too small) | `[00:29:27.050]` |

## 4. Time and place

| Dimension | Rule | His words |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | Prefer a slot like **11 AM** — people are awake, nobody has gone back to sleep, it is not right after a meal, nobody is beat up yet | `[00:10:38.280]` "And the reason is most people at MIT are awake by then,"; `[00:10:48.170]` "It's not right after a meal." |
| **Light** (the first requirement of a room) | The first thing to say to the AV people before you start: **lights full up** | `[00:10:58.546]` "And the most important thing about the place"; `[00:11:29.220]` "is say, keep the lights full up." |
| ↳ rebutting "turn the lights down so the slides show better" | His reply is the most memorable line of the lecture | `[00:11:42.610]` "it's extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids." |
| **Cased** (scout the room) | Go early and look; change what can be changed, learn in advance what cannot | `[00:11:53.070]` "Well, the place should be cased, and I"; `[00:12:15.750]` "the first thing I ask my host to do is to take me to the place" |
| ↳ self-calming trick | Before you go on, imagine every seat filled with a disinterested farm animal | `[00:12:39.203]` "I imagined that all the seats were" |
| **More than half full** | Prefer too small to too big | `[00:13:09.370]` "everyone would be wondering, what's"; `[00:13:17.055]` "doesn't have to be packed, but it" |
| **Counter-example** | A dark hall reached by a catwalk corridor is a talk killer | `[00:34:52.462]` "And then when you get in there, you" |

**These are pure on-site conditions** (for transferability to video / voice-over, see §8.2).

## 5. What to talk about / what not to

### 5.1 ✅ The first **5 minutes** must deliver two things, or you have already lost

> `[00:44:58.000]` "said, they have to show us they've"
> `[00:45:05.940]` "they have to show us that they've done something."
> `[00:45:33.970]` "Five minutes."
> `[00:45:38.470]` "if you haven't told people that you've done something in five"

(The setting is a faculty candidate's job talk — borrow its **strictness**, don't transplant the
criterion wholesale to other settings.)

### 5.2 The two deliverables, defined

| Deliverable | His definition | Timestamp |
|---|---|---|
| **vision** | "a problem that somebody cares about and something new in your approach." | `[00:45:51.250]` |
| **having done something** | **list the steps**, don't list a table of results: "By listing the steps that need to be taken in order to achieve the solution to that problem."; and `[00:47:12.602]` "You don't have to have done all of those steps." | `[00:47:04.990]` |

**The sandwich**: open by listing the steps → blah blah in the middle → close by listing your
contributions, `[00:48:01.110]` "It's kind of mirror of these steps.". The close is a mirror of the open: `[00:47:45.290]` "So then blah, blah, blah, blah, blah, blah, blah, blah.".

### 5.3 ✅ You must **situate** (put the work in context) — one of his two named causes of oral-exam failure

> `[00:42:08.790]` "the most usual reason for people failing an oral exam"
> `[00:42:12.540]` "is failure to situate and a failure to practice."
> `[00:42:22.680]` "This is a problem that's being pursued all over the world."

### 5.4 ✅ You must **inspire** — through **passion**, not adjectives

> `[00:37:38.310]` "And what I saw from everyone is that they were inspired when"
> `[00:37:44.050]` "someone exhibited passion about what they were doing,"
> `[00:38:15.630]` "So when I'm lecturing in my AI class, it's natural for me"
> `[00:38:20.640]` "to talk about what I think is cool"

His survey result, three sources: incoming freshmen were inspired by **someone who told them they
could do it**; senior faculty were inspired by **someone who helped them see a problem in a new
way**; and **everybody** was inspired by **passion** (`[00:37:21.520]` "What I found from the incoming freshmen" → `[00:37:44.050]` "someone exhibited passion about what they were doing,").

### 5.5 ✅ You must **tell stories**: people are storytelling animals

> `[00:40:42.360]` "Well, I believe that we are storytelling animals."
> `[00:41:11.730]` "you provide them with the stories they need to know,"

### 5.6 ✅ Practice in front of **people who don't know what you're doing**

> `[00:42:43.530]` "But that doesn't mean showing your slides to the people"
> `[00:42:48.090]` "you share an with."
> `[00:42:54.023]` "they will hallucinate that there's"
> `[00:43:06.330]` "to help you debug a talk because they, in fact, know"

His rehearsal opener: `[00:43:23.760]` "if you can't make me cry, I won't value as a friend".

### 5.7 ⛔ "Don't talk about other people's work" = **no evidence found**

A full-text search for `other people's work` / `someone else's work` / `don't talk about` /
`their work`: **0 hits**. His actual position leans the opposite way — when you present your own
work you should **build a fence around the idea**:

> `[00:06:38.190]` "I want to build a fence around it so that it's not confused"
> `[00:07:13.990]` "might seem similar to Jones's algorithm,"
> `[00:07:15.740]` "except his is exponential, and mine's linear."

**Conclusion**: you may write "actively draw the boundary against other people's work"; you may
**not** write "Winston says don't talk about other people's work".

## 6. Verbal craft

### 6.1 cycling: go around the same core point **three times**

> `[00:05:56.480]` "Go around it."
> `[00:06:16.190]` "is, at any given moment, about 20% of you"
> `[00:06:22.030]` "So if you want to ensure that the probability that everybody"
> `[00:06:24.530]` "gets it is high, you need to say it three times."

**The number behind it**: at any given moment about **20%** of the room is fogged out → say the
core point three times. He also mocks the "treat the audience as stupid" version of the advice
(`[00:06:05.900]` "Tell him again, and then tell him a third time,"); the real reason is that 20%.

### 6.2 verbal punctuation: give the fogged-out listener a **seam to get back on**

> `[00:07:44.780]` "And the idea here is that, because people"
> `[00:07:48.050]` "will occasionally fog out and need to get back on the bus,"
> `[00:07:52.730]` "you need to provide some landmark places where"
> `[00:08:24.770]` "I'm giving you a sense that there's a seam in the talk,"

**Action**: announce numbers and landmarks out loud ("first … second … third …", "we just did X,
next is Y") so a listener who dropped out knows this is the moment to re-board.

### 6.3 Ask a question, then **stay silent for about 7 seconds**

> `[00:09:11.010]` "I counted seven seconds."
> `[00:09:13.080]` "It seemed like an eternity to me to wait and not say anything"
> `[00:09:23.700]` "It can't be too obvious because then people"

**Two design constraints**: the question can't be too obvious (people are embarrassed to say it)
and can't be too hard (nobody has anything to say). **Pause ceiling: 7 seconds.**

### 6.4 ⛔ Speaking rate and volume: **he gives no number at all** (no evidence found)

A full-lecture search for `words per minute` / `wpm` / `pace` / `slow down` / `speed of speech` /
`volume`: **0 hits**. The only place he discusses "rate" at all is writing speed at the board:

> `[00:14:30.310]` "The speed with which you write on the blackboard"

**Conclusion**: "talking fast" is not the crime, "the audience can't keep up" is; the benchmark is
**delivery rate matching absorption rate**, not "N words per minute". Any claim that "Winston says
speak N words per minute" is invented.

### 6.5 ⛔ Don't step on it: anything that costs you **eye contact** (reading your script / laser pointer / turning your back)

The argument and the full quote set are in §3.5 crime ⑦. His line for that
"the speaker might as well not be here" state: `[00:31:11.790]` "and he wouldn't know.".

## 7. ★ Winston's star: the five S's (each element defined in his words)

**The name**: `[00:50:11.070]` "Well, there's something I like to call Winston's star.".
**What they share**: `[00:50:14.720]` "And every one of the items I'm about to articulate starts".
**Why he talks about it**: his early career-making work "accidentally" had all five elements —
`[00:50:51.630]` "that that work accidentally had all of the elements".
**The motive**: `[00:49:45.240]` "Your ideas are like your children."; `[00:49:38.070]` "You never get used to being ignored.".

| # | Element | His definition | Timestamp | His example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Symbol** | "some kind of symbol associated with your work" | `[00:50:56.220]` (also `[00:50:24.510]`) | the arch itself |
| 2 | **Slogan** | "a kind of phrase that provides a **handle** on the work" | `[00:51:05.510]` | `one shot learning` |
| 3 | **Surprise** | "you don't need a million examples of something to learn. You can do it with one example..." | `[00:51:45.210]` / `[00:51:51.330]` | one example is enough |
| 4 | **Salient idea** | "**when I say salient idea, I don't mean important.** What I mean is an idea that **sticks out**." | `[00:52:13.280]` / `[00:52:16.785]` | `near miss` |
| 5 | **Story** | "you need to tell the story of **how you did it, how it works, why it's important**." | `[00:52:45.490]` | the narrative of the whole paper |

**⚠ The `salient` trap (the single easiest element to misread)**

- It does **not** mean "important". His words: `[00:52:13.280]` "Now, when I say salient idea, I don't mean important.", `[00:52:16.785]` "What I mean is an idea that sticks out."
  → **sticks out / stands out**.
- The negative criterion: `[00:52:21.260]` "Some theses, funnily enough, have too many good ideas," → **too many good ideas = the audience can't tell
  which one you are talking about**.
- When you write it down, write "the one that sticks out", never "the most important point".

**Order (the order he names them in)**: `[00:51:41.490]` "So that's a symbol, slogan." → `[00:52:02.180]` "So that was the surprise." →
`[00:52:07.160]` "Next item was a salient idea." → `[00:52:45.490]` "And finally, you need to tell the story".

### 8.1 ✅ Directly usable on a 100-second voice-over script / card copy (manual mapping)

|---|---|---|---|
| §1.2 | Open with an empowerment promise | the hook segment of a voice-over *is* "what you will know when these 100 seconds are over" | hook |

| §2.1 / §2.2 | Don't end on conclusions, don't say "thanks for watching" | same direction as house rules (no summary segment, no courtesy filler in the CTA) | cta |

| §3.5 crimes ②③④⑤⑥ | few words / no clutter / speaker is the main course / give time to read | applies directly to **card copy**: a wordy card competes with the narration for the single language processor | cards |

| §6.3 | One rhetorical question + one short beat of silence | one question is available in 100 seconds, and the pause must be short (the 7-second ceiling does not apply) | inter-segment design |
| §5.4 | Passion comes from specifics, not adjectives | the "I did X" first-person register | why / use |

| §5.6 | Get reviewers who **have never seen this project** | peers "hallucinate" material that isn't in the script | review process |
| §6.4 | Match delivery rate to absorption rate | set the length from the content, do not cut content to hit a length | length discipline |
| §7 | The five elements in his own words (especially `salient ≠ important`) | calibrates the field criteria | the 5S-related fields |

## 9. Pre-flight checklist (hand-assembled; every line points back to a clause above)

1. Is the first sentence an **empowerment promise** (what they will know at the end)? — not a joke,
   not a self-introduction → §1.1 / §1.2
2. Is there one **cross-domain analogy** landing the abstract idea? → §1.3
3. Has the core point gone **round three times** (open, middle, echo)? → §6.1
4. Is there **numbering / a landmark** to leave a seam for listeners who dropped out? → §6.2
5. Is there one **rhetorical question**, with a short enough pause (ceiling 7 seconds live, shorter
   in voice-over)? → §6.3
6. Has on-screen / card text been cut down to anchors only? (many words on a slide = the audience
   reads and stops listening) → §3.5 crime ⑤
7. Have the cards dropped background, long text, logos and redundant titles? → §3.5 crime ④
8. Are you **reading a script**? Is there anything pulling attention off the speaker? → §3.5 crimes ②⑦
9. Does the close avoid "thank you", avoid conclusions, avoid a long collaborator list? → §2.1 / §2.2
10. Does the close carry an explicit **end signal** (a claim / call to action / echo) rather than
    just fading out? → §2.4
11. Are all five elements present? Is `salient` **sticking out** (not "important")? Are there too
    many good ideas? → §7
12. Has the script been read by **someone who has never seen this project**? → §5.6
