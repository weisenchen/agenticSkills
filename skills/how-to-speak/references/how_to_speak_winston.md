## 1. The thesis: what he is actually talking about

> `[00:00:38.070]` "and that's because your success in life"
> `[00:00:40.340]` "will be determined largely by your ability to speak,"
> `[00:00:43.760]` "your ability to write, and the quality of your ideas,"

That is: **"your success in life will be determined largely by your ability to speak, your ability
to write, and the quality of your ideas — in that order."**

**The capability formula (the ground on which he argues that this is learnable)**

> `[00:00:59.450]` "determined by this formula."
> `[00:01:01.880]` "It's a matter of how much knowledge you have,"
> `[00:01:05.150]` "how much you practice with that knowledge,"
> `[00:01:07.670]` "and your inherent talent, and notice"
> `[00:01:10.100]` "that the T is very small."
> `[00:01:12.230]` "What really matters is what you know."

Read it as K (knowledge) × P (practice) + T (talent), with **T very small**. So "I'm just not a
natural speaker" does not hold — the trainable part is nearly all of it. He draws the conclusion
from a skiing story:

> `[00:01:25.430]` "of the celebrities was Mary Lou Retton,"
> `[00:01:30.800]` "famous Olympic gymnast, perfect 10s in the vault."
> `[00:01:35.480]` "And I heard that she was a novice at skiing,"
> `[00:01:40.070]` "I looked over on the novice slope"
> `[00:01:42.470]` "and saw this young woman who, when she became unbalanced,"
> `[00:01:53.570]` "But then, it occurred to me, I'm a much better skier"
> `[00:02:03.230]` "And I was a better skier because I had the K, and I had the P,"
> `[00:02:08.120]` "and all she had was the T. So you"

**His promise (the "deliverable" of the lecture)**

> `[00:02:17.460]` "So that's what my objective is today, and here's my promise."

> `[00:02:24.830]` "of what you can put in your armamentarium of speaking"
> `[00:02:28.400]` "techniques, and it will be the case"
> `[00:02:32.810]` "that some one of those examples, some heuristic, some technique,"
> `[00:02:37.640]` "maybe only one, will make--"
> `[00:02:40.040]` "will be the one that gets you the job."
> `[00:02:43.050]` "And so this is a very non-linear process."
> `[00:02:45.600]` "You never know when it's going to happen,"

### 1.1 His repeatedly stated method: build your own repertoire

> `[00:09:43.460]` "And now, if this persuades you that there"
> `[00:09:47.340]` "is something to know, that there is knowledge,"
> `[00:09:52.620]` "then I've already succeeded because what I want to convince"
> `[00:09:55.500]` "you of, is if you watch the speakers you admire and feel"
> `[00:09:59.160]` "are effective, and ask yourselves"
> `[00:10:01.140]` "why they're successful, then you can"
> `[00:10:03.030]` "build up your own personal repertoire"
> `[00:10:06.000]` "and develop your own personal style."

## 2. How to open

### 2.1 Rule A: **do not open with a joke** (his position is unambiguous)

> `[00:04:25.470]` "Some people think the right thing to do"
> `[00:04:28.740]` "is to start a talk with a joke."
> `[00:04:39.530]` "I don't recommend it, and the reason"
> `[00:04:44.300]` "is that, in the beginning of a talk,"
> `[00:04:46.820]` "people are still putting their laptops away."
> `[00:04:49.420]` "They're becoming adjusted to your speaking parameters,"
> `[00:04:52.250]` "to your vocal parameters, and they're not ready for a joke."
> `[00:04:58.670]` "So it doesn't work very well."
> `[00:05:00.090]` "They usually fall flat."

**The reasoning, unpacked from his words**: at the start the audience is still putting laptops
away and still adjusting to your **vocal parameters**. A joke requires them already to be on the
bus, and at that moment they are not. **The result is that it falls flat.**

### 2.2 Rule B: **open with an "empowerment promise"**

> `[00:05:02.420]` "What you want to do instead is start with empowerment promise."
> `[00:05:09.560]` "You want to tell people what they're"
> `[00:05:11.060]` "going to know at the end of the hour"
> `[00:05:13.660]` "that they didn't know at the beginning of the hour."
> `[00:05:16.560]` "It's an empowerment promise."
> `[00:05:17.808]` "It's the reason for being here."

**His own demonstration (this lecture opens with exactly that move)**

> `[00:05:21.660]` "What would be an example?"
> `[00:05:22.910]` "Oh, I see."
> `[00:05:24.230]` "At the end of this 60 minutes, you will know things"
> `[00:05:28.820]` "about speaking you don't know now,"
> `[00:05:30.952]` "and something among those things you know"
> `[00:05:32.660]` "will make a difference in your life."
> `[00:05:34.310]` "Yeah, that's an empowerment promise,"
> `[00:05:36.200]` "so that's the best way to start."

**An earlier, fuller version of the promise** — the structure is "promise + probability statement +
criterion (one of these will get you the job)" — see the quotes at the end of §1, from
`[00:02:17.460]` onward.

### 2.3 Rule C: **set a rule of engagement** at the start and enforce it on the spot

> `[00:03:11.900]` "Now, in order to do that, we have"
> `[00:03:14.300]` "to have a rule of engagement, and that"
> `[00:03:17.150]` "is no laptops, no cell phones."
> `[00:03:22.710]` "So if you could close those, I'll"
> `[00:03:24.600]` "start up as soon as you're done."
> `[00:03:27.300]` "Some people ask why that is a rule of engagement,"
> `[00:03:31.800]` "and the answer is, we humans only"
> `[00:03:34.830]` "have one language processor."
> `[00:03:40.590]` "If your language processor is engaged browsing the web"
> `[00:03:44.850]` "or reading your email, you're distracted."
> `[00:03:48.030]` "And worse yet, you distract all of the people around you."
> `[00:03:50.880]` "Studies have shown that."
> `[00:03:52.570]` "And worse yet, if I see a open laptop somewhere back"
> `[00:03:56.160]` "there or up here, it drives me nuts, and I do a worse job."
> `[00:04:02.470]` "And so that ensures that all of your friends"
> `[00:04:04.710]` "who are paying attention don't get the performance"
> `[00:04:08.520]` "that they came to have."

**Why he imposes it**: `"we humans only have one language processor"` (`[00:03:34.830]` "have one language processor.") — occupy
it with a web page and you drop out, and **you also take everyone around you out**; and if someone
in the room has a laptop open, the speaker himself does a worse job
(`[00:03:52.570]` "And worse yet, if I see a open laptop somewhere back" → `[00:04:08.520]` "that they came to have.").
> ★ **This mechanism is the substrate of the whole lecture, and it reappears as crime ⑤ of the
> slide section (§4.5)**: many words on a slide = occupying the audience's one language processor.

### 2.4 Rule D: you may open with a **cross-domain analogy** (his actual opening move)

He does not talk abstractly about "grabbing attention" — he opens with a **military-law analogy**:

> `[00:00:19.220]` "PATRICK WINSTON: The Uniform Code of Military Justice"
> `[00:00:23.840]` "specifies court martial for any officer who sends a soldier"
> `[00:00:27.530]` "into battle without a weapon."
> `[00:00:30.620]` "There ought to be a similar protection for students"
> `[00:00:32.995]` "because students shouldn't go out"
> `[00:00:34.370]` "into life without the ability to communicate,"

An officer who sends a soldier into battle without a weapon faces a court martial; likewise a
student should not go out into life without the ability to communicate. **The analogy lands first,
the thesis follows.**

## 3. How to close

### 3.1 Rule A: **the final slide = `contributions` (what you have done), not `conclusions`**

He rejects five kinds of "ending slide" one by one, then gives the right one:

> `[00:53:53.730]` "Nice piece of work, but not so much,"
> `[00:53:55.770]` "but it's only a tiny piece of work if you divide by 1,000."
> `[00:54:01.050]` "So when you show a whole gigantic list of collaborators"
> `[00:54:03.810]` "at the end of a talk, it's a kind of let down"
> `[00:54:07.380]` "because it suggests that nobody knows."
> `[00:54:09.150]` "Well, did you do anything significant?"
> `[00:54:12.650]` "Now, you've got to recognize your collaborators, right?"
> `[00:54:15.180]` "So where do you do that?"
> `[00:54:16.970]` "Not on the last slide, on the first slide."
> `[00:54:19.877]` "All this was on the first slide."
> `[00:54:21.210]` "These are the collaborators, so you don't"
> `[00:54:23.263]` "want to put them at the end."
> `[00:54:24.430]` "You don't want a slide like this."

> `[00:54:28.290]` "How about this one?"
> `[00:54:31.060]` "This is the worst possible way to end a talk."
> `[00:54:33.050]` "[LAUGHTER]"
> `[00:54:34.700]` "Because this slide can be up there for 20 minutes."

> `[00:54:51.777]` "What about this one?"
> `[00:54:56.780]` "I often see it."
> `[00:54:57.800]` "I never see anybody write it down."
> `[00:55:01.300]` "Also, it wastes opportunity."
> `[00:55:06.580]` "Oh my God, even worse."

> `[00:55:23.730]` "with who you are."

> `[00:55:26.360]` "Well, what about this?"

> `[00:55:31.450]` "It might seem so at first, but here's the problem."

> `[00:55:44.910]` "What they care about is what you have done."
> `[00:55:47.557]` "And that's why your final slide should"
> `[00:55:49.140]` "have this label, contributions."
> `[00:55:57.830]` "And the final slide, the one that's"
> `[00:55:59.460]` "up there while people are asking questions and filing out,"
> `[00:56:02.240]` "it ought to be the one that has your contributions on it."

**Note**: he also rules "a long list of collaborators on the final slide" a failure — the correct

**What his own contributions slide looks like**

> `[00:56:09.140]` "Yeah, this is what I talk about a lot."

> `[00:56:23.146]` "And the final element there is this is what we get out of it,"
> `[00:56:27.616]` "so that's an example of a contribution slide."

### 3.2 Rule B: **do not make "thank you" your last sentence**

> `[00:57:50.770]` "Thank you."
> `[00:57:54.030]` "I don't recommend it."
> `[00:57:55.650]` "It's a weak move."
> `[00:57:58.410]` "You will not go to hell if you conclude"
> `[00:58:00.330]` "your talk by saying thank you, but it's a weak move,"
> `[00:58:02.730]` "and here's why."
> `[00:58:04.880]` "When you say thank you, even worse, thank you for listening,"
> `[00:58:08.460]` "it suggests that everybody has stayed"
> `[00:58:10.350]` "that long out of politeness and that they had a profound desire"
> `[00:58:14.310]` "to be somewhere else."
> `[00:58:15.632]` "But they're so polite, they stuck it out."
> `[00:58:17.340]` "And that's what you're thanking them for."
> `[00:58:20.430]` "So once wild applause has started,"
> `[00:58:21.990]` "you can mouth a thank you, and there's"
> `[00:58:24.240]` "nothing wrong with that."
> `[00:58:25.380]` "But the last thing you do should not be saying thank you."

**He anticipates the objection and rejects it**

> `[00:58:29.992]` "Now, you say to me, well, doesn't everybody say"
> `[00:58:31.950]` "thank you?"
> `[00:58:33.570]` "Well, what everybody does is not necessarily the right thing."
> `[00:58:37.800]` "And I like to illustrate how some talks can"
> `[00:58:40.110]` "end without saying thank you."

### 3.3 Rule C: a joke **is** allowed at the end (the opposite of the opening!)

> `[00:56:50.140]` "One thing you could do in the final words"
> `[00:56:54.700]` "is you could tell a joke."
> `[00:56:59.840]` "It's OK."
> `[00:57:02.232]` "By the time you're done, people have adjusted themselves"
> `[00:57:04.565]` "to your voice parameters."
> `[00:57:05.728]` "They're ready for a joke."
> `[00:57:10.040]` "I was sitting in another bar, this time in Austin, Texas"
> `[00:57:13.010]` "with a colleague of mine named Doug Lenat."
> `[00:57:16.310]` "And Doug's a fantastic speaker."
> `[00:57:18.880]` "And so I said to Doug, Doug, you're"
> `[00:57:21.920]` "a fantastic speaker, what's your secret?"
> `[00:57:24.860]` "And he said, oh, I always finish with a joke,"
> `[00:57:29.440]` "and that way, people think they've had fun the whole time."
> `[00:57:35.480]` "So yeah, a joke will work down there."

**Mechanism**: by the end the audience **has already tuned to your vocal parameters**
("people have adjusted themselves to your voice parameters") — the same reason as §2.1, seen
from the other side.

### 3.4 Rule D: the ending must carry an explicit **"the event is over" signal**, not a courtesy

He gives two political examples (a benediction) and "salute the audience":

> `[00:59:08.966]` "CHRIS CHRISTIE: And together, everybody, together."

> `[00:59:27.260]` "God bless you, God bless America."

> `[01:00:09.390]` "PATRICK WINSTON: Now, watch this."
> `[01:00:11.410]` "Let's go back a little bit and redo it."
> `[01:00:13.297]` "What I want you to see is that at one point,"
> `[01:00:15.130]` "he seems to be almost pressing his lips together, forcing"
> `[01:00:18.430]` "himself not to say thank you."
> `[01:00:20.315]` "Then there's another place where he does a little salute."
> `[01:00:22.690]` "So watch for those this time around."

> `[01:00:39.625]` "PATRICK WINSTON: That's where he's pursing his lips."
> `[01:00:43.186]` "There's the salute."

> `[01:00:50.830]` "Now, what are we gonna take away from this?"
> `[01:00:52.640]` "Well, I suppose I could conclude this talk by saying God bless"
> `[01:00:57.910]` "you, and God bless the Massachusetts Institute"
> `[01:01:00.960]` "of Technology, but it might not work so well."
> `[01:01:04.732]` "But what you can get out of this is"

**The "end signal" is a man-made convention, not something innate — he gives two everyday
examples**

## 4. Tools: board / props / slides

### 4.1 General principle: **pick the tool by purpose — board for informing, slides for exposing**

> `[00:13:24.550]` "Next thing I want to talk about is a subject"
> `[00:13:27.640]` "of boards and props and slides."
> `[00:13:38.230]` "Well, these are the tools of the trade."
> `[00:13:41.230]` "I believe that this is the right tool for speaking"

### 4.6 That slide's "diagnosis → prescription" (crimes 4.5 collapsed into one executable table)

For the man on his way to Europe to give a job talk, he gave **only two lines of diagnosis**:
"You have too many, and they have too many words." (`[00:25:08.610]` "You have too many, and they have too many words."), and "My reply was,
because it's always true. There are always too many slides, always too many words."
(`[00:25:19.800]` "My reply was, because it's always true." → `[00:25:22.770]` "There are always too many slides, always too many words."). **He had not looked at the deck — one glance was
enough**, because he treats this as the **general case**, not an exception.

Two lines of diagnosis, but the prescription can be laid out crime by crime:

| What he found | What he prescribed | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Slides/word count out of control | Accept "it's always true", then **cut slide by slide** | §4.5 crime ① |
| Reading the slides | Don't; the audience can read, and reading annoys them | §4.5 crime ② |
| Speaker and screen at opposite ends | Slides are the **condiment**, the speaker is the main course | §4.5 crime ③ |
| Background / logo / title | Four-step delete: background → words → logo → title | §4.5 crime ④ |
| Audience reads, doesn't listen | Remember there is **only one language processor** | §4.5 crime ⑤ |
| Font too small | Keep a sample slide to measure the **legibility floor (~40–50 pt)** | §4.5 crime ⑥ |
| Laser pointer | Don't; use **an arrow plus a number** | §4.5 crime ⑦ |
| Deck too heavy | **Print it and lay it on a table**: is there air / white space / imagery? | §4.5 crime ⑧ |
| A deliberately incomprehensible graphic | **One per presentation** (hapax legomenon) | §4.5 crime ⑨ |

> `[00:08:28.040]` "and you can get back on."

**The move is simply to announce numbers and seams** ("first … second … third …") so the audience
knows this is the moment to come back.

### 7.3 **Ask a question — and wait through 7 seconds of dead air**

> `[00:08:33.299]` "So now, we're on a roll, and since we're on a roll,"
> `[00:08:38.130]` "can you guess what fourth idea might be here--"
> `[00:08:42.270]` "an idea that helps people get back on the bus?"
> `[00:08:48.800]` "AUDIENCE: Ask a question."
> `[00:08:51.405]` "PATRICK WINSTON: Yes?"
> `[00:08:52.280]` "AUDIENCE: Ask a question."
> `[00:08:53.322]` "[INAUDIBLE]"
> `[00:08:53.860]` "PATRICK WINSTON: Ask a question, yes."
> `[00:08:55.570]` "Thank you."
> `[00:08:58.220]` "So ask a question."
> `[00:09:05.420]` "And so I will ask a question-- how much dead air can there be?"
> `[00:09:09.030]` "How long can I pause?"
> `[00:09:11.010]` "I counted seven seconds."
> `[00:09:13.080]` "It seemed like an eternity to me to wait and not say anything"
> `[00:09:16.300]` "for seven seconds, but that's the standard amount of time"
> `[00:09:19.740]` "you can wait for an answer."
> `[00:09:20.910]` "And of course, the question has to be carefully chosen."
> `[00:09:23.700]` "It can't be too obvious because then people"
> `[00:09:25.620]` "will be embarrassed to say it, but the answers can't"
> `[00:09:28.410]` "be too hard because then nobody will have anything to say."

**The question's design constraints**: not too obvious (the audience would be embarrassed to say
it), not too hard (nobody would have anything to say). **Pause ceiling: 7 seconds** — he says those
seven seconds felt like an eternity.

### 7.4 Speaking rate and volume: **he gives no number at all — measured, so don't invent one**

A full-lecture search for `words per minute` / `wpm` / `pace` / `slow down` / `speed of speech` /
`volume`: **0 hits** (`pace` occurs twice, neither in the sense of speaking rate). The only place he
talks about "rate" is §4.2:

> `[00:14:30.310]` "The speed with which you write on the blackboard"
> `[00:14:32.440]` "is approximately the speed at which people can absorb ideas."
> `[00:14:36.220]` "If you go flipping through a bunch of slides,"
> `[00:14:39.040]` "nobody can go that fast."

Read it as: **"talking fast" is not the crime, "the audience can't keep up" is**; his prescription
is to make the **delivery rate** match the **absorption rate**. **His benchmark is "the speed you
write at the board", not "N words per minute"** — any claim that "Winston says speak N words per
minute" is invented.

### 7.5 One thing not to step on: anything that costs you eye contact (reading your script, laser pointer, back to the audience)

The argument and the full quote set are in **§4.5 crime ⑦** (`[00:30:41.190]` → `[00:31:41.730]`).
Only his single line for "the speaker might as well not be there" is repeated here:

> `[00:31:11.790]` "and he wouldn't know."

**What this means for scripted voice-over**: a voice-over script is written for the ear, but the
video-side equivalent of "losing contact" is **the narration being stolen by the picture** — two
faces of the same sentence.

### 7.6 Verbal-craft checklist

| # | Action | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cycle the core point three times — because ~20% are fogged out at any moment | §7.1 |
| 2 | Use numbering / "first, second, third" as verbal punctuation, leaving a seam for the fogged-out | §7.2 |
| 3 | Ask a question where appropriate; **wait in silence about 7 seconds** | §7.3 |
| 4 | The question must be neither too obvious nor too hard | §7.3 |
| 5 | Match the delivery rate to the absorption rate (writing speed at the board is the standard) | §7.4 |
| 6 | Avoid anything that costs you eye contact (reading a script, laser pointer) | §7.5 |

**Definition in his words**: surprise = **a claim that overturns the other person's existing

### 8.5 Element ④ **Salient idea** — the definition most easily misread

**Definitions in his words (copy these exactly):**

- negative criterion: `"Some theses, funnily enough, have too many good ideas, and you don't know

## 10. Where this hooks into a scripted workflow: what applies directly, what is on-site only

A 100-second spoken technical explainer (narration + cards) is **not** a live lecture, so the split
matters:

### 10.1 ✅ Directly applicable (do it when writing an explainer / voice-over)

| Clause | Winston's rule | Why it fits a 100-second voice-over | Where it lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| §2.2 | **Empowerment-promise opening** | the `hook` segment is exactly this — "what you will know when these 100 seconds are over" | `segments[hook]` |
